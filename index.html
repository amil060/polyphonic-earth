        How the sonification works

        Each frame of the NASA visualization is drawn into a hidden canvas and analysed pixel by pixel (192 × 96 grid). The pixel colours are converted back into data values using the visualization's own colour scale. Before the music starts, a sound legend plays the lowest and the highest value once, like the axis of a chart. A slow sweep then moves from west to east once per bar. Each of the 8 slices of the map becomes a note: its value sets the pitch, how much of it contains data sets the loudness, and its position sets the stereo pan. The layers enter one at a time, and you can switch each one on or off. Notes use quarter-tone maqam scales (Rast, Saba, Hijaz): the value picks a scale degree and what is left over bends the note by up to 25 cents, so small differences in the data stay audible. In the CO₂ scene the chord's two voices drift apart in cents as concentration rises, and you hear them beat. A soft knock marks a slice with no data, for example land in the ocean scene.

        About the project

        The problem

        NASA's Earth Information Center shows how our planet is changing through beautiful visualizations, but only to people who can see them. More than 2.2 billion people live with a vision impairment (WHO), and many more learn better by listening.

        Our answer

        Polyphonic Earth reads every frame of NASA visualizations and NASA POWER data and plays them as music in real time. Pitch, loudness, rhythm and stereo carry the data; descriptions, Listen by Touch and a visual mode make it usable with or without sight or hearing.

        Why accessibility

        Climate data belongs to everyone. Designing for blind and deaf visitors first gave us features that help every listener: a sound legend before the music, layers that enter one at a time, and plain explanations of what each sound means.

        Team

        Open Guys AI, NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026, Mingachevir, Azerbaijan. The start-screen theme song is an original composition by our teammate Ali Aliyev. Built with open NASA data, Tone.js and an AI coding assistant; the concept, data choices, sound design decisions and testing are ours.

        What's next

        1. Test with blind and low-vision listenersin Azerbaijan, then change what does not work for them. So far we have tested with keyboards and automated checks, not yet with a screen-reader user.
        2. A museum modefor science centres and planetariums: headphones, one big button, and the 60-second tour on a loop.
        3. More NASA datasuch as sea ice, vegetation and sea level, each with its own instrument.
        4. An offline versionfor classrooms without fast internet.
